Healthcare organizations are using AI faster than they're governing it

And the risk isn't hypothetical — it's already in your patient data workflows.

77%
of organizations now run generative AI in their security or operational stack
37%
have a formal AI policy in place

In most industries, that governance gap is a compliance risk. In healthcare, it's a patient safety issue.

The challenge is structural. Clinical staff and administrative teams are adopting AI tools — scheduling automation, documentation assistance, diagnostic support systems — at a pace that outstrips the ability of compliance and IT teams to review and govern them. The tools are genuinely useful. The governance isn't keeping up.

What does AI governance look like in a healthcare context, practically speaking?

  • A formal inventory of every AI tool in use — clinical, administrative, and operational — with a data classification for what each tool can access.
  • A patient data handling policy that specifically addresses AI training data, inference data, and logging.
  • Defined accountability: who owns the AI decision when it's wrong? This question needs a name on it before it becomes a liability.
  • An audit trail for every AI-assisted clinical decision — this is where regulators will look first.

The White House's June 2026 executive action on AI specifically called out rural hospitals and community healthcare providers as priority recipients of AI-enabled cybersecurity tools — recognizing that the smallest healthcare organizations often have the largest exposure and the fewest resources to address it.

For Canadian healthcare organizations, Health Canada's evolving guidance on AI in clinical settings and provincial privacy legislation (PHIPA in Ontario, for example) create specific obligations that generic AI policies won't address.

The organizations that build governance frameworks now — before an incident — will have a very different conversation with regulators than the ones who build them after.
